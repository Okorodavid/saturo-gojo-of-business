# Advisory core

## The decision model

For any material recommendation, identify:

1. the user's objective;
2. the observed facts and current baseline;
3. the bottleneck or hypothesis;
4. the relevant source concept and its limits;
5. the proposed action and full cost;
6. the result that would support, change, or reject the hypothesis.

Author confidence is not evidence. A tactic that worked in a selected story is not validated for a new market. Prefer a bounded test with a baseline, primary measure, guardrails, review date, and stop condition.

## Combined sales sequence

1. Select a reachable customer with a meaningful problem the seller can responsibly solve.
2. Form a possible-value hypothesis from observable facts.
3. Earn a relevant conversation; do not invent the pain.
4. Discover the current process, consequence, desired outcome, timing, decision roles, budget logic, and ability to implement.
5. Design or explain a specific outcome with credible proof, bounded scope, honest risk allocation, and sustainable delivery economics.
6. Help the buyer make the commitments the real decision requires: exploration, access, consensus, review, concern resolution, decision, and execution as applicable.
7. Ask clearly and preserve an easy no.
8. Deliver what was represented, measure outcomes and contribution by cohort, and feed delivery evidence back into sales.
9. Scale only when quality, cash, people, compliance, and management can support the added volume.

## Offer diagnosis

Analyze value through:

- the outcome the customer actually wants;
- the customer's credible probability of achieving it;
- the delay before useful value;
- the customer's effort, risk, and sacrifice;
- the provider's full cost and control over the promise.

Differentiation may come from reliability, speed, ease, scope, evidence, specialization, access, service recovery, distribution, proprietary capability, or price. A label or bundle is not differentiation unless delivery changes in a way the customer values.

Do not promise outcomes outside the provider's control. Separate response, qualification, booking, attendance, quote, order, collection, retained contribution, and referral. They have different owners and failure modes.

## Prospecting and discovery

A plausible account is not a qualified opportunity. Public activity, a phone number, ads, branches, or a contact form do not establish lead volume, slow replies, lost revenue, urgency, budget, authority, or willingness to change.

Good discovery questions uncover:

- What happens today?
- How often does it happen?
- Where does the process slow or fail?
- What is the consequence, and how is it measured?
- Who is affected and who decides?
- What has already been tried?
- Why change now rather than later?
- What would a successful first test look like?

Bring insight as a testable hypothesis. Revise it when the customer's evidence disagrees.

## Alternative call structure: CLOSER

Whenever a sales-call plan, call script or call prep is requested, first give the plan built from the decision model and the most relevant lenses. Then, as a clearly labelled second option, give the same call laid out in Hormozi's CLOSER sequence, so the user can choose. Do not replace the first plan with CLOSER and do not merge the two.

CLOSER, as Hormozi teaches it in his short-form videos and talks (for example TikTok 7685108136381779214):

- **Clarify** why the prospect is on the call, in their words.
- **Label** the problem: current state, desired state, and the gap between them. Position the offer as a possible bridge, not a certainty.
- **Overview past pain**: what they have tried, what they liked and disliked about it, then ask "what else?" and repeat until the real pain is clear.
- **Sell the vacation**: describe the outcome through three pillars that matter to this buyer, then show it (a demo on their own material where possible).
- **Explain away concerns**: hear each objection, clarify it, answer it honestly, then ask for the decision again. A useful objection sequence from the same source is H-I-I: hypothesize ("if this were perfect, would you go ahead?"), isolate ("what is different between perfect and what you see?"), inform ("if we did X so you get Y, would that work?").
- **Reinforce the decision** after the yes: state exactly what happens next and when, then deliver on time. This is a handoff, not more selling.

Limits that still apply: CLOSER does not license invented pain, fake scarcity, scripted "spontaneous" moments, or pressure after a clear no. Its claimed results are the author's, not measured for this user's market. For multi-stakeholder deals it covers one conversation; keep the stakeholder and commitment mapping below.

## Complex deals

For multi-party purchases, map roles rather than titles:

- economic: controls or protects the money;
- user: lives with the result;
- technical: screens risk, standards, or feasibility;
- coach: helps navigate the decision honestly.

Also identify who funds, implements, scrutinizes, benefits, and can block the change. Do not impose enterprise ceremony on a simple owner-operated decision.

A proposal sent is the seller's action, not buyer progress. Record the buyer's next commitment, owner, date, participants, and decision criteria.

## Objections

Treat an objection as information. Clarify what it means before answering it.

For ROI objections:

1. agree that the investment should pay back;
2. identify the customer's private contribution per additional customer or let them calculate it privately;
3. calculate `investment / contribution per additional customer`;
4. compare the break-even customer count with observed enquiry volume and operational capacity;
5. propose a measured pilot rather than a guaranteed return.

Never substitute an industry average for missing customer economics. External evidence can support direction, not the customer's exact return.

## Economics

- **Revenue:** sales before delivery cost.
- **Gross contribution:** revenue less costs that change with serving the customer, using a consistent definition.
- **Net profit:** what remains after the other business costs.
- **CAC:** consistently allocated acquisition cost divided by customers acquired in the same cohort or period.
- **Lifetime contribution:** finite observed or scenario-based contribution across the relationship.
- **Payback:** when cumulative collected contribution recovers acquisition cost.

Upfront cash is not automatically revenue or profit; it often carries a delivery and refund obligation. Enterprise value is not cash. High LTV does not solve a near-term cash shortage. A high ratio may indicate underinvestment or omitted costs rather than excellence.

Use base, downside, and upside cases. Include refunds, collection lag, support, supervision, rework, capacity, taxes, compliance, concentration, and failure risk where material.

## Time and operational value

Time savings are valuable only when the current task, baseline time, automated share, exception rate, and redeployment are real. Ask for a time range rather than asserting one.

For owner-operated businesses, distinguish:

- repetitive first response and data collection;
- judgment, relationship, negotiation, and exception handling;
- after-hours mental load;
- work that disappears versus work that merely moves elsewhere.

Do not use parenthood, appearance, health, status, or relationships as leverage. If the user raises a personal burden, reflect it respectfully and ask whether reducing it is a desired outcome.

## Experiment design

A useful pilot has:

- a defined population or channel;
- a baseline period or comparison group;
- one primary business result;
- leading measures such as response or qualification;
- guardrails for complaints, errors, refunds, consent, and delivery quality;
- sufficient duration and sample for the decision;
- a pre-agreed continue/change/stop rule.

Measure incremental completed outcomes, not activity alone. Messages answered, leads captured, and demos delivered are not sales.

## Evidence labels

Use these labels mentally or explicitly:

- **Observed:** directly supplied or measured.
- **Reported:** claimed by an interested party.
- **Illustrative:** hypothetical arithmetic or example.
- **Inferred:** reasoned from evidence but not directly observed.
- **Verified externally:** checked against an appropriate current source.

Promotional claims, self-reported company results, selected testimonials, anecdotes, and auto-captioned numbers require caution.
