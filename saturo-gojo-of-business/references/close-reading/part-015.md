# The New Strategic Selling: part-015 close reading

Scope: all twelve assigned chunks of remaining text/OCR from PDF1–179 of *The New Strategic Selling*, including cover, publication material, contents, endorsements, full client list, workshop instructions and chart OCR. Previously credited pages are deliberately absent. This package ends during the discussion of intangible Wins, not at the end of the book. Every acknowledgement followed full consumption of its emitted chunk. Page locators below are PDF pages. Source success claims are not independently verified.

## Strategy is a revisable account model

The preface distinguishes principles, process, practice and discipline (PDF10–14). Principles explain why a move matters; process gives a sequence; real-account workshops let learners apply it; management and users must sustain use. Miller explicitly credits customers' discipline rather than a magical seminar. The 2004 preface, older Marriott foreword and late-1990s revision explanation coexist, so corporate figures and client status should be read in their historical frames. The extensive client list (PDF22–41) demonstrates claimed reach across industries, not causal proof of the revenue increases surrounding it. The list itself warns that publishing delays make currency uncertain.

The revision explanation (PDF17–21) is substantive. It adds degrees of influence, a clearer bridge between corporate Results and personal Wins, a competition chapter, and a reordered relationship between ideal-customer qualification and funnel management. The authors apply their adapt-or-decline principle to their own successful product. That is more useful than treating a sales method as a timeless script immune to feedback.

A Complex Sale is defined structurally: several people must provide approval or input (PDF47–49). Price and technical sophistication are not decisive; basketballs sold to a chain can require more strategic work than a small transaction involving complex merchandise. The framework is expressly less indispensable for single-person counter or door-to-door selling. This scope qualification matters when translating it to an owner's small business: complexity depends on actual decision dependencies, not on ambitions or deal size alone.

The Ray/Greg case (PDF44–46) supplies the central failure mechanism. Ray knows the CEO, purchasing and users, but misses the division manager's actual approval authority and a trusted outside consultant. Greg maps this sale's decision structure, uses the consultant's credibility and wins. The lesson is that social prestige and an impressive contact list do not prove coverage. The stronger claim that every lost done deal always reflects failure to apply the right process exceeds what this anecdote establishes. Process reduces uncertainty; it does not remove all competition, chance or genuine product disadvantages.

Chapter2 distinguishes pre-call positioning from in-call tactics while insisting they are interdependent (PDF59–65). Strategy gets the seller to the appropriate people at an appropriate time; encounters then supply feedback that changes strategy. An unnamed manufacturer declines to launch an assembly customers eagerly want because operation and service burdens would damage relationships (PDF63). That gives concrete content to account-centered selling: a possible order can be a strategic loss. Military metaphors are explicitly limited; buyers are not enemies to beat.

## Make uncertainty visible before acting

The four-step account process is current-position analysis, alternate positions, selection/action planning and implementation, followed by continual review (PDF65). The first workshop makes this manageable by choosing one real account with unresolved uncertainty rather than an abstract case (PDF66–75). It lists environmental changes, classifies current threat/opportunity perceptions and links them to a Single Sales Objective. That objective specifies what, quantity and expected timing. “One objective” does not prohibit pursuing multiple opportunities; it prevents blending opportunities with different decision networks into one vague account story.

The Euphoria-Panic Continuum is explicitly descriptive, not predictive (PDF72–74). Feelings can signal neglected information, but neither elation nor anxiety establishes deal probability. False positivity may hide trouble, while panic produces indiscriminate activity. The productive response is to identify what is unknown and what action would improve the position. This is a meaningful corrective to motivational texts that imply confidence itself changes buyer facts. The workshop's own use of subjective feelings is framed as a starting point to be supplemented, not a replacement for evidence.

The six-element overview (PDF76–87) introduces Buying Influences, Red Flags/Strengths, Response Modes, Win-Results, Ideal Customer and Sales Funnel as an interacting system. The last two appear here largely as previews, so detailed conclusions about their later implementation would exceed this package. Their intended role is clear: avoid bad-fit prospects, and balance selling work to prevent a revenue roller coaster. The asserted thirty-five percent poor-prospect figure lacks a supplied method or denominator; the underlying qualification principle does not depend on that exact percentage.

## Roles and coverage precede organizational labels

The role-based approach (PDF78–79,94–115 including OCR addenda) distinguishes economic approval/release of funds, users' job impact, technical screening and coaching guidance. One person can fill multiple roles; many people can fill user or technical roles. The economic role may be a person or set of people, so the repeated shorthand “one Economic Buyer” should be interpreted as one final-approval locus for the specific objective rather than always one human. Titles alone cannot establish that locus.

The airline-reorganization example (PDF101) reveals an external technical veto: creditors, court and unions agree, but the FAA's previously reassigned slots prevent the plan. Screening power can exist outside the obvious customer organization. Technical contacts may also misunderstand delegated recommendations as final authority because their executives phrase instructions ambiguously (PDF101–102). That explanation usefully avoids assuming every misstatement is deliberate deception.

Coaches differ from the other roles because they must be developed, not merely located (PDF102–103). Their purpose is to interpret the situation and guide progress on this particular objective. A friendly contact is not automatically a Coach. Degree of influence also varies with organizational impact, expertise, location, personal priority and internal politics (PDF110–114). The text cautions that likely is not certain; a remote executive may still wield decisive influence. Coverage can be performed by the seller or someone better placed. Personally meeting everyone is not the sole allowed strategy, but knowing a name without anyone qualifying that person's position leaves a base uncovered.

Red Flags mark missing or uncertain information, uncovered bases, new players and reorganization (PDF115–131, including the PDF122 OCR list). Finding a flag is progress rather than failure. Strength is defined narrowly: differentiation the buyer values, relevant to the current objective, that improves the seller's position. A superior warm jacket has no inherent strategic value in a Caribbean sale. This turns generic feature claims into testable, contextual advantages.

The finance example (PDF125–127) compares hammering an inaccessible executive, ignoring that executive, and working through an enthusiastic user. Coaching or an introduction uses existing credibility to resolve the uncovered economic role. It is not a guarantee: reaching the executive may reveal that productivity is not a priority (PDF130). Alternate positions must therefore remain specific, inclusive while being developed, and tested for their ability to use strength or reduce a flag. The output is a changing options list, not a rigid master plan.

## Receptivity is not personality or friendship

Response Modes describe how a person sees the proposed change now (PDF132–158). Growth perceives a gap calling for more/better; Trouble perceives deviation requiring repair; Even Keel sees no gap; Overconfident perceives reality beyond the desired result. The chart OCR at PDF138–146 was consumed alongside the assigned text. The same person can change modes and different participants can view the same proposal differently. Liking the seller is also different from supporting this proposal. Growth or Trouble may favor a competitor, while a friendly Even Keel buyer may have no reason to act.

The minus-five-to-plus-five assessment has no zero and must be checked against actual willingness to support or resist (PDF154–157). Uncontacted people should be flagged rather than assigned optimistic guesses. The absence of a zero is a modeling convention, not evidence that uncertainty or neutrality cannot exist. Internal consistency tests are useful: a purported Coach who is not supportive is mislabeled, and a positive score paired with an unreceptive mode needs investigation.

Even Keel can sometimes shift through credible evidence of a discrepancy, relevant growth/protection or another influence's intervention (PDF144–146). Often watchful waiting is wiser. The QC-system case (PDF148–149) illustrates this: a supervisor dismisses warnings while scaling fifty boards daily to five hundred, then seeks help when returns reach twenty-seven percent. The broad claim that overconfidence always inevitably becomes Trouble is more categorical than the evidence warrants. Likewise, describing dissent as perceptual illness risks converting a situational model into a judgment of the person, contrary to earlier cautions. Buyer perceptions matter to adoption, but the rhetorical claim that only perceptions and no reality exist should not be taken literally; operational results still require verification.

## Win-Win must survive delivery and each person's experience

The matrix (PDF160–172) concerns the seller's relationship with each influence, including after the order. Winning is satisfaction of perceived self-interest, distinct from selfish disregard of others. Selling an inflated urgent offer, overstating service or delivering the wrong sophistication can leave a buyer losing even without an illegal act. The textile-user retaliation and editor losing a later book illustrate why ignored individuals can affect future business (PDF166–167). Those cases make long-term consequences vivid, though not every unhappy buyer necessarily seeks revenge.

Seller sacrifice is also examined. Discounts and free services may create unrealistic future expectations. Importantly, the authors qualify their sweeping claim that Lose-Win always collapses: an introductory concession can be a legitimate short-term tactic if its limits are explicit and preferably written (PDF168–169). Thus a discount is not inherently a loss; sustainable economics, shared understanding and transition terms matter.

The assigned end distinguishes an objective Result, such as sixteen-percent overtime reduction, from whether Doris Green values it personally (PDF178–179). A shared business improvement may threaten someone's income or preferred way of working. Wins are subjective, culturally influenced and change over time. Inferring them from demographics alone would defeat the demand to understand the individual. Compared with selective study of four buyer labels, the full assigned remainder reveals a broader discipline: specify one decision, expose uncertainty, test influence and receptivity, develop alternate moves, and protect mutual value after signing. No workshop instruction here authorizes outreach or records an owner decision.
